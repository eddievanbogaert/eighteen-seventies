# Calandro on Grant and the Panic of 1873

Notes on a secondary source. Nothing here is verified; this file records what
the article argues, what it is good for, and what may not be taken from it.

## The source

Joseph Calandro, "Ulysses S. Grant and the Panic of 1873," *Quarterly Journal
of Austrian Economics* 28, no. 1 (11 June 2025). DOI 10.35297/001c.137926.
Retrieved 2026-11-13; sha256 `4f577a2173fd0ac5…`. Citation details are the
publisher's own metadata, not recalled.

Manifest key `calandro-grant-panic-1873`, labelled a position rather than a
neutral account — the same treatment the manifest already gives Davis on the
famine and Desmond and Moore on Darwin.

## What it argues

Its own abstract states the thesis without hedging: the post-war boom ended in
the panic of 1873, and "responses by Ulysses Grant, which were consistent with
Austrian economic prescriptions, effectively mitigated the effects of the
panic."

So the paper is an argument that Grant was right, with rightness measured
against a particular school's prescriptions. That is a legitimate thing for a
paper to be. It is not a neutral narrative of the panic and must never be
cited as one.

The treatment of Grant is warm to the point of advocacy. A passage noting that
Grant understood Gresham's law takes the trouble to add that this "points to
an atypical level of national financial understanding for a president," then
contrasts him with John F. Kennedy, who reportedly could not distinguish
"monetary" from "fiscal". That comparison does no work on the 1870s. It is
there to raise Grant's standing, and it is a reliable signal about how the
rest of the evidence is being weighed.

## What it is genuinely good for

**As a finding aid, it is valuable.** It points at documents this project
wants and did not know to look for:

- The Currency Bill, also called the Inflation Bill, as **Senate Bill No. 617**.
- Its passage by both houses and transmission to Grant on **14 April 1874**;
  his veto announced in cabinet on **21 April 1874**, with a written message
  to Congress the following day.
- *The Papers of Ulysses S. Grant* (ed. Simon, 1967–2009) as the edition to
  work from, with specific volume and page pointers: vol. 25, pp. 73–75 for
  the veto; vol. 25, p. 116 for a remark of 1 June 1874 on poorer currency
  driving out better; vol. 25, p. 272 for a passage from a draft annual
  message of 7 December 1874.

**As a position, it is citable under rule 4.** Chapter 10 has to record how
historians disagree about the panic, and this is a clearly stated, recently
published, attributable position by a named author in a named journal. That is
exactly what rule 4 asks for. The chapter can say what Calandro argues and
who else argues otherwise.

## What may not be taken from it

**Its primary facts are second-hand and sometimes third-hand.** The House vote
on the Currency Bill is given as 105–64 among Republicans with Democrats
against 35–37, and that is quoted from Rothbard (2002, 151) rather than from
the record. Rothbard's own source is not given here. A vote tally that reaches
this project through two intermediaries is a pointer, not a figure. Rule 2
applies: those numbers do not enter a chapter until they are read in the
Congressional Globe or the House Journal.

**Its quotations of Grant need checking against the Grant Papers.** The
edition is scholarly and the volume and page references are specific, which
makes checking easy. That is a reason to check, not a reason to skip it.

**Its framing is the thing the book has a rule against.** The section on the
1874 Currency Bill opens not in 1874 but in 2007–08, with the Federal Reserve
and quantitative easing, and the comparison carries argumentative weight
rather than decorating the page. The claim is not merely that the episodes
rhyme; it is that the same error recurred and Grant avoided it.

This is worth noticing for its own sake. `thesis.md` confines present-day
material to the introduction, a coda, and at most one deliberate passage per
thread, on the grounds that a parallel is framing and never evidence. Here is
a published paper in a peer-reviewed journal doing precisely what that rule
forbids, in a field adjacent to this book's, and the result is that a reader
cannot easily tell where the history stops and the advocacy begins. The rule
is not fastidiousness. This is what it is for.

## The structural finding, which matters more than the argument

`voice-and-shape.md` records that the chapter list has a hole in the middle of
the decade: four tentpoles in 1873, one in 1874, none in 1875, four in 1876.

This article puts two money-thread events straight into that hole. **The
Inflation Bill and Grant's veto, April 1874. The Specie Resumption Act,
January 1875.** Both are federal monetary legislation, both sit on the thread
the book's thesis leads with, and the second is the statute that chapter 21's
whole subject depends on — Q21-1 asks what act mandated resumption, when it
passed and what it required, and the answer is apparently a law of 1875 that
the chapter list does not currently contain anywhere.

That is the useful thing this source did, and it did not require trusting a
word of its argument.

**Proposed, for the author:** the 1875 Resumption Act is not a candidate for a
new tentpole so much as a missing piece of an existing one. Chapter 21 is
dated `1879-01` and treats the day resumption took effect; the act that
mandated it is four years earlier. Either the chapter's span opens to
1875–1879, or the act gets its own chapter in the empty year. The second would
fix the chronological hole and give the money thread an event in 1875. See
`chapter-order.md`.
