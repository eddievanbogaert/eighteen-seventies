The Coinage Act of 1873 is a piece of administrative housekeeping. Its
sections run consecutively to sixty-seven [C07-013], and a reader working
through them meets a great deal about who works at the mint and what they are
paid. It establishes the mint as a bureau of the Treasury Department,
under a director appointed by the President with the Senate's consent, for a
five-year term [C07-005]. It names the mints — Philadelphia, San Francisco,
Carson, Denver — and the assay-offices at New York, Boise City, and Charlotte
[C07-012]. It fixes the proportion of pure metal to alloy at nine hundred
parts in a thousand [C07-009]. It sets salaries for the superintendents, assayers, melters,
refiners, coiners and engravers by name and amount, and provides that wages be
paid in monthly instalments [C07-014].

Somewhere in the middle of this, in a subordinate clause, the United States
goes onto the gold standard.

## The sentence

Section 14 lists the gold coins: a one-dollar piece, a quarter-eagle, a
three-dollar piece, a half-eagle, an eagle, a double eagle. Then it gives
their weights in grains. The one-dollar piece appears first, and the clause
attached to it reads: *which, at the standard weight of twenty-five and
eight-tenths grains, shall be the unit of value* [C07-007].

That is the whole of it. No preamble, no declaration, no section of its own.
The phrase sits inside an enumeration of coin weights, under a marginal note
that says only "Gold coins;". A reader working through the act in order
encounters the monetary constitution of the United States somewhere between
the salary of the engraver at Philadelphia and the standard weight of the
double eagle.

Section 14 goes on to make the gold coins a legal tender in all payments
[C07-008].

## The omission

Section 15 does the same job for silver. It lists the silver coins of the
United States: a trade-dollar, a half-dollar, a quarter-dollar, a dime
[C07-002]. It gives their weights — the trade-dollar at four hundred and
twenty grains troy [C07-006] — and makes them legal tender at their nominal
value for any amount not exceeding five dollars in any one payment [C07-003].

The standard silver dollar is not on the list [C07-002].

Three sections later the act says why that matters, in terms that leave
nothing to inference. Section 20 provides that an owner of gold bullion may
take it to any mint "to be formed into coin or bars for his benefit"
[C07-024]. Section 21 provides that an owner of silver bullion may take it to
be formed into bars, or into trade-dollars — "and no deposit of silver for
other coinage shall be received" [C07-023].

That is the end of free silver coinage, stated in a sentence, in the enacted
text. After February 1873 a man with a bag of silver could still walk into the
Philadelphia mint. He could come out with bars, or with trade-dollars, and
with nothing else. A man with a bag of gold could come out with money.

So the asymmetry is in the act twice. Once in sections 14 and 15, in what the
coins are and what they are legal tender for; once in sections 20 and 21, in
what the mint will take across the counter. Section 17 then forbids issuing
any coin not set out in the act [C07-004], and section 67, the last, gives the
act its name — it is the statute itself, not posterity, that calls it the
Coinage act of eighteen hundred and seventy-three — and repeals all prior law
inconsistent with it [C07-010].

What is remarkable about the Coinage Act is not that it hid what it was doing.
It is where it put it. The sentence that ends free silver coinage sits in a
run of sections about depositing bullion, between one on the devices stamped
upon coins and one on the minimum weight of a bar. A reader looking for the
monetary policy of the United States would not think to look there, and a
legislator skimming a codification of mint administration had no particular
reason to either.

## Why this is hard to see, and why that matters

Sections 14 and 15 are the gold standard stated in full. Gold is the unit of
value and legal tender in all payments. Silver is a list of subsidiary coins,
legal tender up to five dollars [C07-008]. After February 1873 a creditor owed
ten thousand dollars could be paid in gold without limit, or in silver up to
five dollars and then no further.

A reader who has got this far may feel the argument is too neat — that
hindsight has arranged a set of clauses into a design. It is worth being
precise about what is and is not being claimed. Everything above is a reading
of the enacted text, section by section, from the page. None of it is a claim
about intention. Whether the men who voted for the bill understood that the
omission in section 15 would do what it did is a separate question, it is
genuinely contested, and this draft cannot answer it, because the debates have
not been read.

What can be said without a source is narrower, and it is a point about the
document rather than about the politics that followed. The act nowhere states
its own effect. A reader who wanted to know, in February 1873, whether the
United States had just adopted a single gold standard would have had to hold
section 14 against section 15, notice an absence in a list, and follow section
17 and section 67 to see that the absence had been sealed. That is not a
difficult inference. It is, however, an inference, and the statute does not
make it for you.

Whether the men who voted understood it is Q07-3, and it stays open here.

## What the chapter still needs

Set down plainly, because the gaps are the honest part of the draft.

The legislative history is half-read, which is worse than unread because it
looks like progress. The Globe's own index gives the skeleton: the measure was
a House bill, H. R. No. 2934 [C07-015]; on the Senate side it was reported at
page 203, amendments reported at 363, read at 661, discussed at 668, and
passed with amendment at 674 [C07-016]; it went to conference, and the
conference report was concurred in by both chambers before enrolment
[C07-017].

Page 668 has now been read, and it is worth more than the index suggested.

On 17 January 1873 Senator Sherman rose to move that the Senate take up the
mint bill. He told the chamber it "will not probably consume any more time
than the time consumed in reading it"; that it had passed the Senate two years
before "after full debate"; that officers of the Government had said it was
important it should pass promptly; and that the points of difference between
the two Houses could go to a conference committee "without having a
controversy here in the Senate about them" [C07-018].

What the Senate then did, as the page records it, was to insert the words "or
sample of bullion" after "coins"; to strike the word "wording" and insert
"working"; and to insert the word "assay" before "office". Sherman described
the batch he sent to the clerk as being "of a formal character" [C07-019].

One amendment drew argument. It was on section fourteen, and it concerned
whether gold coins worn below a stated weight by ordinary use should be
received and recoined at the Government's expense. Senator Cole thought the
House language wise and hoped it would stay [C07-020].

Section fourteen is the section containing the clause that makes the gold
dollar the unit of value. The Senate argued about abrasion.

Silver is not mentioned anywhere in the mint-bill proceedings printed on that
page [C07-021].

That last sentence has to be handled carefully, and the chapter should handle
it in the open. It is a statement about one page. The proceedings run on past
it, the House took the bill up again four days later [C07-022], and a thing's
absence from a page is weaker evidence than its presence on one. What page 668
establishes is not that nobody understood. It is something narrower and, for
this chapter, more useful: on the day the Senate considered the bill, the
recorded business was a misspelling, two insertions, and a dispute about worn
gold coins — and the measure was commended to the chamber as one that need not
detain it.

The historiography is unread. Q07-3 is a rule 4 case: the positions must be
named and attributed, and not one of the works in the manifest that bears on
it has been acquired. Until then this chapter records a mechanism and declines
a verdict.

The price series is unread. What happened to world silver production and the
silver price around 1873, and which way the causation runs, is Q07-4.

And the international frame is missing entirely. Q07-5 asks which other
countries changed monetary standard in the same years and whether any source
treats them as one connected episode. This is the thesis-critical one: the
book argues that the 1870s installed the operating system of the modern world,
and a chapter in which the United States does this alone is a chapter that
quietly makes the argument American.

  find the sentence. No adjective does work the statute can do itself. This is
  the "pleasure of the mechanism" test in `voice-and-shape.md` and it is the
  single best case for it in the book — if this does not work here, the voice
  is wrong.

  `thesis.md` is unspent, and this is not the place for it.
- **The pushback paragraph is deliberate.** A reader's suspicion that the
  argument is too tidy is correct scepticism and gets answered directly rather
  than written around.

  named, characterised, and left open, because the sources that would settle
  it are unread. The received nickname for the controversy is deliberately not
  used: it needs a source, and it has not got one yet.
