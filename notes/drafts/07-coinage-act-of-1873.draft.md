# Chapter 07 — draft, partial

The Coinage Act of 1873 is a piece of administrative housekeeping. Its
sections run consecutively to sixty-seven [C07-013], and a reader working
through them meets a great deal about who works at the mint and what they are
paid. It establishes the mint as a bureau of the Treasury Department,
under a director appointed by the President with the Senate's consent, for a
five-year term [C07-005]. It names the mints — Philadelphia, San Francisco,
Carson, Denver — and the assay-offices at New York, Boise City, and Charlotte
[C07-012]. It fixes the proportion of pure metal to alloy at nine hundred
parts in a thousand [C07-009]. It sets salaries for the superintendents, assayers, melters,
refiners, coiners and engravers by name and amount, and provides that wages be
paid in monthly instalments [C07-014].

Somewhere in the middle of this, in a subordinate clause, the United States
goes onto the gold standard.

## The sentence

Section 14 lists the gold coins: a one-dollar piece, a quarter-eagle, a
three-dollar piece, a half-eagle, an eagle, a double eagle. Then it gives
their weights in grains. The one-dollar piece appears first, and the clause
attached to it reads: *which, at the standard weight of twenty-five and
eight-tenths grains, shall be the unit of value* [C07-007].

That is the whole of it. No preamble, no declaration, no section of its own.
The phrase sits inside an enumeration of coin weights, under a marginal note
that says only "Gold coins;". A reader working through the act in order
encounters the monetary constitution of the United States somewhere between
the salary of the engraver at Philadelphia and the standard weight of the
double eagle.

Section 14 goes on to make the gold coins a legal tender in all payments
[C07-008].

## The omission

Section 15 does the same job for silver. It lists the silver coins of the
United States: a trade-dollar, a half-dollar, a quarter-dollar, a dime
[C07-002]. It gives their weights — the trade-dollar at four hundred and
twenty grains troy [C07-006] — and makes them legal tender at their nominal
value for any amount not exceeding five dollars in any one payment [C07-003].

The standard silver dollar is not on the list [C07-002].

It is not abolished. It is not mentioned. There is no sentence in the Coinage
Act of 1873 declaring that the silver dollar shall cease to be struck, for the
same reason that there is no sentence declaring the adoption of the gold
standard. The work is done by what is absent from an enumeration, and by two
other provisions that close the gaps around it.

Section 17 provides that no coins of gold, silver or minor coinage shall
thereafter be issued from the mint other than those of the denominations,
standards and weights set forth in the act [C07-004]. Section 67, the last,
gives the act its name — it is the statute itself, not posterity, that calls
it the Coinage act of eighteen hundred and seventy-three — and repeals all
other acts and parts of acts pertaining to the mints, assay-offices and
coinage that are inconsistent with it [C07-010].

Put the three together. The standard silver dollar is absent from the list of
silver coins; no coin outside the list may be issued; and prior law
inconsistent with the act is repealed. There is no longer any route by which a
holder of silver bullion could bring it to the mint and have it struck into
legal-tender dollars [C07-011].

## Why this is hard to see, and why that matters

The asymmetry between sections 14 and 15 is the gold standard stated in full.
Gold is the unit of value and legal tender in all payments. Silver is a list
of subsidiary coins, legal tender up to five dollars [C07-008]. After February
1873 a creditor owed ten thousand dollars could be paid in gold without limit,
or in silver up to five dollars and then no further.

A reader who has got this far may feel the argument is too neat — that
hindsight has arranged a set of clauses into a design. It is worth being
precise about what is and is not being claimed. Everything above is a reading
of the enacted text, section by section, from the page. None of it is a claim
about intention. Whether the men who voted for the bill understood that the
omission in section 15 would do what it did is a separate question, it is
genuinely contested, and this draft cannot answer it, because the debates have
not been read.

What can be said without a source is narrower, and it is a point about the
document rather than about the politics that followed. The act nowhere states
its own effect. A reader who wanted to know, in February 1873, whether the
United States had just adopted a single gold standard would have had to hold
section 14 against section 15, notice an absence in a list, and follow section
17 and section 67 to see that the absence had been sealed. That is not a
difficult inference. It is, however, an inference, and the statute does not
make it for you.

Whether the men who voted understood it is Q07-3, and it stays open here.

## What the chapter still needs

Set down plainly, because the gaps are the honest part of the draft.

The legislative history is unread. Who drafted the silver provisions, what the
debates addressed, how long passage took — Q07-2, and it requires the
Congressional Globe for the third session of the Forty-Second Congress, which
has not been retrieved.

The historiography is unread. Q07-3 is a rule 4 case: the positions must be
named and attributed, and not one of the works in the manifest that bears on
it has been acquired. Until then this chapter records a mechanism and declines
a verdict.

The price series is unread. What happened to world silver production and the
silver price around 1873, and which way the causation runs, is Q07-4.

And the international frame is missing entirely. Q07-5 asks which other
countries changed monetary standard in the same years and whether any source
treats them as one connected episode. This is the thesis-critical one: the
book argues that the 1870s installed the operating system of the modern world,
and a chapter in which the United States does this alone is a chapter that
quietly makes the argument American.
