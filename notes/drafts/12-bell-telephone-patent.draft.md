On the evening of 10 March 1876, in a house in Boston, a man shouted a
sentence into a brass pipe standing in a bowl of acidulated water [C12-011]
[C12-012]. In the next room, behind two closed doors, his assistant pressed
one ear against a flat steel armature and stopped the other with his hand
[C12-013]. The sentence got through. The assistant came, and said so, and
repeated it back [C12-015].

Three days earlier, the United States Patent Office had granted the shouting
man a patent on the method [C12-003].

That ordering is the whole chapter, and it is not a scandal. It is how the
nineteenth century's most consequential piece of intellectual property
actually came into the world: described on paper first, demonstrated
afterwards, contested almost immediately, and settled — to the extent it ever
was — by lawyers rather than by anyone listening to anything.

## What the patent said

Patent 174,465 is titled *Improvement in Telegraphy* [C12-001]. The word
"telephone" does not appear in it. This is not coyness; it is an accurate
description of what Bell thought he had. The problem he had been paid to solve
was how to push more than one telegraph message down a single wire at a time,
and the invention he describes is a way of making an electrical current
undulate — vary smoothly, like a sound wave in air — rather than merely
stutter on and off.

The patent lists several ways to produce such a current. One of them is to
vary the resistance of the circuit itself, and the specification offers an
instance: let mercury or some other liquid form part of the circuit, and the
resistance falls as the wire is pushed deeper in [C12-008]. The fourth of the
patent's five claims covers that method in general terms — producing
undulations by gradually increasing and diminishing resistance — and mentions
no liquid at all [C12-007] [C12-005].

The fifth and last claim is the one that mattered. It covers transmitting
vocal or other sounds telegraphically, by causing electrical undulations
similar in form to the vibrations of the air carrying the sound [C12-006]. In
fourteen printed lines, at the end of a document about telegraphy, is the
telephone.

A reader meeting this for the first time usually wants to know how a patent
office could hand over so much on so little. The answer is that it did not
feel like much at the time. The claim is a method, stated at the level of
physical principle, in a field where dozens of people were filing methods. It
became enormous later, when it turned out that the principle had only one
commercially viable embodiment and Bell owned it.

## The dates, in the order the documents give them

The specification was signed on 20 January 1876 [C12-004]. The application
reached the Patent Office on 14 February [C12-002]. The patent issued on
7 March [C12-003]. The instrument that carried a sentence through a closed
door was built on the morning of 10 March and tried that evening [C12-011].

Twenty-five days separate Bell's signature from his filing [C12-004]. That
interval is the hinge on which a century of argument turns, because on the
same day the application was filed, Elisha Gray lodged a caveat at the same
office describing apparatus for transmitting vocal sounds — and the question
ever since has been whether anything travelled from Gray's document into
Bell's.

**This chapter cannot answer that question, and says so plainly.** Gray's
caveat has not been read. No digitised copy could be found; the accounts of it
that are easy to reach are tertiary, and a drawing that reached this project
without any statement of where it came from has been set aside rather than
used — see `notes/pending-provenance.md`. Until the document is in hand, what
can honestly be said is this: Bell's patent contains a liquid passage, the
liquid passage sits in the specification rather than in any claim [C12-007]
[C12-008], and the allegation that it arrived there late is an allegation this
book has not tested.

## The instrument that worked was the contested one

Here the notebook and the patent touch, and the touch is worth slowing down
for.

The thing Bell built on 10 March was a liquid transmitter. His own diagram
labels it: a brass pipe, a platinum wire, standing in liquid under a
mouthpiece [C12-012]. It is the variable-resistance method, which is to say it
is the method of the fourth claim, whose worked instance in the specification
is the mercury passage [C12-007] [C12-008].

So the apparatus that first carried speech was built on the principle that the
priority dispute is about. That is a fact about the documents, and it is the
reason the dispute has never gone quiet. It is not, by itself, evidence of
anything improper.

## The page nobody quotes

The famous sentence has a problem, which is that it is famous. Set beside the
pages around it, it is the least interesting thing in the notebook.

Read the entry for the previous day. On 9 March, Bell had a membrane stretched
over a box, a cork, a wire dipping into water in a glass vessel [C12-018]. He
heard something. He thought he could make out a voice counting — and then he
stopped himself, and wrote that it might have been fancy, because he knew
beforehand what to expect [C12-017].

That is a man building a control into his own laboratory notebook.

And on the 10th, having got his sentence through, he immediately ran the
experiment the other way. He and Watson changed places, Watson read an unknown
passage, and Bell listened. He could not follow it. He caught "to", and "out",
and "further", and then one sentence arrived whole [C12-016].

The record, in other words, is not a record of triumph. It is a record of a
careful man refusing to be fooled by his own instrument, including on the
evening it worked. A chapter that quotes only the shouted sentence has quoted
the one page where he let himself be pleased.

It also gets the sentence wrong. What Bell wrote was *Mr. Watson — Come here —
I want to see you*: spaced dashes, a capital on Come. When he recorded Watson
repeating it back a moment later, he wrote it lower-case [C12-014]. Neither of
the versions that circulate — the tidy comma'd one, the variant ending "I want
you" — is on the page.
