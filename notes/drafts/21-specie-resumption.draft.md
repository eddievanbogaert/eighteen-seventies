# Chapter 21 — draft, partial

**This is not the chapter.** It lives in `/notes` because every claim it rests
on is `verified: "n"`. See `EXCEPTIONS.md` E-1.

Partial, and it stops where the sources stop. Q21-4 asks what actually
happened on and after the effective date, and that has not been read. A
chapter about a day cannot end before the day; this draft ends four years
early, on purpose.

Claim markers are left in. `tools/reading_copy.py` strips them.

---

The most consequential day in the decade's monetary history was fixed four
years in advance, by a subordinate clause, in a section about something else.

On 14 January 1875 Congress approved an act to provide for the resumption of
specie payments [C21-001]. Its third section is mostly concerned with the
circulating notes of national banks: it repeals the statutory ceiling on how
many of them there may be, and sets up a machinery by which the Treasury
retires greenbacks as the banks issue paper, eighty dollars withdrawn for
every hundred put out, until three hundred million dollars of United States
notes remain and no more [C21-005].

Then, without a new section or a heading or any change of tone, the sentence
arrives:

> And on and after the first day of January, anno Domini eighteen hundred and seventy-nine, the Secretary of the Treasury shall redeem, in coin, the United States legal-tender notes then outstanding on their presentation for redemption, at the office of the assistant treasurer of the United States in the city of New York, in sums of not less than fifty dollars. [C21-002]

Four years and eleven months later, a man with fifty dollars in paper could
walk into an office on Wall Street and walk out with gold.

## What the act does not say

It does not say how. This is the striking thing about it once the shape comes
clear: **the date is mandatory and the means are permissive.** The Secretary
*shall* redeem on that day. To get ready, he *may* use any surplus revenue not
otherwise appropriated, and he *may* sell bonds under the refunding act of
1870, at not less than par, in coin. The act repeals a ceiling and authorises
a borrowing power and then stops.

Congress, in other words, did not legislate resumption. It legislated a
deadline and left four years for the executive branch to make the deadline
true. Everything that happened in the Treasury between 1875 and 1879 happened
in the space that section three left empty.

## What it finishes

Two years earlier, the Coinage Act had closed the mint to silver. It did so in
a clause about bullion deposits: a man with silver could have bars, or
trade-dollars, and "no deposit of silver for other coinage shall be received"
[C21-013], while a man with gold could deposit it "to be formed into coin or
bars for his benefit" [C21-014].

Section 2 of the Resumption Act finishes the thought. It repeals the charge of
one fifth of one per cent that the mint had been taking for converting
standard gold bullion into coin, and provides that no charge shall be made for
that service thereafter [C21-003].

So: from January 1875, bringing gold to the mint was free, and bringing silver
was not possible. Neither provision announces itself. The first is a sentence
about what the mint will accept over the counter; the second is a repeal of a
fee. Between them they describe a monetary system, and neither uses the word
for it.

## The year the thing nearly went the other way

Resumption was not inevitable in 1875, and the proof is that it had been
beaten a year earlier.

In April 1874 Congress sent Grant a currency bill which would have expanded
the circulation. He returned Senate Bill No. 617 without his approval
[C21-006]. The veto held.

What is interesting about the message is the ground he took. He might have
argued that inflation would fail on its own terms. Instead he argued that the
thing had been promised: in his annual messages, in each inaugural address, in
a House resolution of December 1865 carried by 144 votes to 6, in the first
act of the Forty-First Congress [C21-007]. The case is not that cheap money
would not work. The case is that the United States had said it would pay, and
had said so repeatedly, and that the saying was the obligation.

The draft in his own hand shows him sharpening that case as he wrote. Where he
had written "if not in every Annual Message", he struck the hedge and left "in
every Annual Message". A clause conceding that his first message was the main
one, and that nothing since had been inconsistent with it, is struck out
entirely [C21-008]. Whether that is a man clarifying his position or hardening
it is a question the manuscript poses and does not answer.

One caution the chapter must carry: what survives in the published papers is
Grant's draft, not the message as sent to the Senate [C21-009]. The two have
not been compared here.

## Where this draft stops

On 1 January 1879 the clause came due.

What happened is Q21-4 and it has not been read. The question is phrased in
the repository with unusual care — "a non-event is a finding, but it has to be
documented as one rather than asserted for effect" — and that caution is
well placed, because the shape of this chapter invites a particular ending and
the evidence for it has not been gathered.

The chapter also cannot yet say what the greenback-to-gold price did as the
date approached, which is Q21-3 and which the repository rightly calls this
chapter's load-bearing quantitative evidence. Nor what the contemporary press
made of it, which is Q21-6.

Until those are read, this is a chapter about a law that set a date, and about
the four years of silence the law left in the middle of itself.

## Drafting notes, not for the chapter

- **The ending is deliberately missing.** Every instinct of the material
  points toward a particular ending — the famous day on which nothing
  happened, because everyone knew the notes were as good as gold. That ending
  may well be right. It is also exactly what a writer would compose if they
  had not read the sources, and the chapter must not reach it by inference.
  Q21-3, Q21-4 and Q21-6 are the work.
- **Opens on the clause, not on the day.** Per `chapter-order.md`, chapter 21
  should run 1875-1879 and begin with the act. This draft assumes that.
- **The link to chapter 07 is load-bearing and should survive editing.** The
  two statutes do the same work from opposite sides, two years apart, and
  neither names what it is doing. That is the money thread's argument in
  miniature.
- **No present-day passage.** The money thread's one allowance is unspent and
  `thesis.md` D-4 is open.
- **Word count:** about 1,000. The budget has this as a standard chapter at
  ~5,000.
