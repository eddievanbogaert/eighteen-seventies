The first edition gives nothing away. A reader who picked the book up in the
spring of 1872 found a title that was almost a word, an anagram a half-second
late in arriving, and under it no author's name at all: *Erewhon, or Over the
Range*, two epigraphs, and the imprint of Trübner & Co. of Paternoster Row
[C04-006]. Turning the page did not help. The preface is one sentence long,
and it is about pronunciation — the Author wishes it to be understood that
Erewhon is pronounced as a word of three syllables, all short [C04-007]. Then
the narrative begins, in a sheep country unmistakably like the one its
anonymous author had lately been living in, and goes on for a hundred and
ninety pages before it arrives at the thing the book is now remembered for.

What it arrives at, on page 190, is a chapter called "The Book of the
Machines" [C04-008]. Three chapters, in fact, and the first of them opens by
putting the whole argument in quotation marks: *The writer commences*. The
narrator is not making the case. He is translating a treatise by a professor
in a country where the machines were abolished, and the case runs as follows.
Consciousness is a matter of degree and of time. The vapour engine has none
that we can detect, but neither, once, did anything else. Machines are
changing faster than animals ever did. They are already fed, tended and
reproduced by a race that believes itself their master. It is a question of
time.

That argument is the reason this book is in a history of the 1870s. It is
also, as it happens, nine years older than the book.

## Cellarius

On 13 June 1863, the *Press* of Christchurch, in the province of Canterbury,
New Zealand, printed a letter to the editor under the heading "Darwin Among
the Machines". It was signed *Cellarius* [C04-009].

Two qualifications belong here rather than in a footnote. The *Press* of
1863 has not been read for this book; what has been read is a London reprint
of 1914, edited by R. A. Streatfeild, fifty-one years and at least one
intervening edition downstream of the newspaper [C04-009]. And the name on
the letter is a pseudonym. That Cellarius was Butler is the word of his
literary executors, published a decade after his death; it is not seriously
contested, and it is still their word and not a signature [C04-009].

With those stated, the letter is the Book of the Machines in miniature and in earnest. It
proposes a mechanical kingdom that stands to the animal as the animal stands
to the vegetable; it works through watches and clocks and the engines of the
*Great Eastern*; it reaches the conclusion that man is building his own
successor and will end as the machines' domestic animal, better fed than he
is now. And then, where the novel will be ironic, the letter is not. It
closes with a programme:

> Our opinion is that war to the death should be instantly proclaimed against
> them. Every machine of every sort should be destroyed by the well-wisher of
> his species. [C04-010]

If that is impossible, the letter adds, the impossibility proves the case:
the mischief is already done, and we are not merely enslaved but acquiescent
in our bondage [C04-010].

Two years later, on 29 July 1865, the same newspaper printed an essay called
"Lucubratio Ebria" — a drunken night-thought — which argues the exact
opposite. Machines are not a rival kingdom. They are limbs. The creature that
learned to carry a stick became "not only a vertebrate mammal, but a
vertebrate machinate mammal into the bargain", and every invention since has
been another limb bolted on [C04-011]. The essay names the view it is
rejecting:

> It is a mistake, then, to take the view adopted by a previous correspondent
> of this paper, to consider the machines as identities, to animalise them
> and to anticipate their final triumph over mankind. [C04-012]

The previous correspondent of that paper was Cellarius, and Cellarius was
Samuel Butler, and so was the man rebutting him.

## What the novel does with the quarrel

Butler could have chosen. Writers settle their minds between drafts all the
time, and the second position is the more sophisticated one, the one that
sounds like a modern philosopher of technology rather than a frightened
colonist. He did not choose. He put both of them in the novel.

The limbs argument turns up in chapter XXIII, given to a second Erewhonian
writer, and the borrowing is close enough to be verbatim in places. There is
the umbrella described as an organ; there is the memory that goes in a
pocket-book; there is the really well-developed specimen of the race
furnished with a large box upon wheels, two horses and a coachman. There is
the proposal that men be reckoned by their horse-power, and that those alone
possess the full complement of limbs who stand in the first rank of opulence
— in the essay, "the Rothschilds are the most astonishing organisms that the
world has ever yet seen"; in the novel, the great merchants and bankers
[C04-013].

And then Butler decides the argument, in one clause, against himself:

> the other writer was considered to have the best of it, and in the end
> succeeded in destroying all the inventions that had been discovered for the
> preceding 271 years [C04-014]

The other writer is the 1863 one. The machines go. What follows the victory,
the same sentence reports, is reactionary civil wars that nearly ruined the
country [C04-014] — which is the kind of detail a satirist puts in and a
prophet leaves out.

## What this is evidence of

It is tempting to read all this as foresight, and the temptation should be
resisted in both directions. Butler's machines are not computers. They are
engines and watches, and the mechanism he imagines for their consciousness is
evolutionary rather than computational: nothing in the Book of the Machines
calculates. The resemblance to arguments now being conducted in public is
real, it is the reason this chapter exists, and it is not evidence of
anything about the 1870s.

What the documents do establish is harder and more useful. The argument
existed, fully formed, in a colonial newspaper in 1863. It reached a London
readership in 1872 in a book that declined to put a name on its title page.
And the man who made it was unable to hold it: he published it, repudiated it
in print two years later, and then, seven years after that, staged his own
repudiation as a loser's speech. He appears to have found the argument both
irresistible and untrue, and to have been honest enough to print the
condition rather than resolve it.

The usual summary of Butler — that he saw the machine age coming — is not
wrong so much as too simple. He saw it coming, and did not believe his own
eyes, twice, in public.
