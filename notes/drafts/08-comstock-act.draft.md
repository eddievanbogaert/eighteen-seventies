The law that would govern what Americans could put in an envelope for the
next century has no name. In the statute book it is chapter 258, one of
several dozen chapters approved on 3 March 1873, and its title is a
description rather than a label: *An Act for the Suppression of Trade in, and
Circulation of, obscene Literature and Articles of immoral Use* [C08-001].
"Comstock Act" came later, from outside. Nobody voting on it used the phrase,
because at the time it was not one.

It sits on the page between an act fixing the face value of Texas and Pacific
Railroad bonds and an act creating a board of local inspectors of steam
vessels for the customs district of Michigan, with a list of new post roads —
Huntington to Wayne Court House, Miller's Ferry to Fayetteville — a few
inches above [C08-007]. That is the end of a congressional session, and it
looks like every other end of a congressional session.

## What the act can reach

The first section is a single sentence of about two hundred words, and its
first dozen do most of the work. Whoever, it begins, *within the District of
Columbia or any of the Territories of the United States, or other place
within the exclusive jurisdiction of the United States*, sells or lends or
gives away or exhibits or possesses for those purposes any obscene book,
picture, print, or article of an immoral nature [C08-002].

That clause is a confession of limits. In 1873 the federal government had no
general police power over what was sold in Philadelphia. It could legislate
for the District, for the Territories, for navy yards and arsenals — places
where it was the only government there was. Inside that space the penalty was
severe: hard labour in the penitentiary, six months to five years, for each
offence, or a fine of a hundred to two thousand dollars [C08-002].

Outside it, the act had to find another handle, and the handle it found was
the mail.

## The amendment

Section 2 does not enact a mail prohibition. It rewrites one: *That section
one hundred and forty-eight of the act to revise, consolidate, and amend the
statutes relating to the Post-office Department, approved June eighth,
eighteen hundred and seventy-two, be amended to read as follows* [C08-004].

Nine months earlier Congress had tidied up the postal code, as legislatures
periodically do. One section of that tidying is now opened and replaced, and
what goes into it is a list: obscene publications, and also *any article or
thing designed or intended for the prevention of conception or procuring of
abortion*, and also any card or circular saying where such things may be
obtained, and also any envelope bearing scurrilous epithets. None of it shall
be carried in the mail [C08-004].

The penalty attached to the rewritten postal section is heavier than the one
in the act's own first section: a hundred to five thousand dollars, or one to
ten years at hard labour, or both, at the judge's discretion [C08-005]. The
same object, sent through the post rather than sold in a territory, carried
twice the maximum sentence.

Two things follow, and they are worth separating. The first is
constitutional: a federal government that could not reach a bookshop could
reach everything the bookshop needed to advertise or ship, which is most of
what a nineteenth-century mail-order trade consisted of. The second is
administrative. The prohibition now lived inside the Post Office's own code,
which meant it would be applied, day after day, by postal employees deciding
what to carry — not by prosecutors deciding what to charge.

## Contraception is in the text

It is sometimes said that the act's reach into contraception was the work of
later courts and later enforcement. It was not. *Any drug or medicine, or any
article whatever, for the prevention of conception, or for causing unlawful
abortion* is in section 1, enacted, in the same breath as obscene pictures
[C08-003]. The postal section says it again [C08-004].

The qualifier "unlawful" sits in front of "abortion" and does not sit in
front of "the prevention of conception". Whether that asymmetry was
deliberate, and what it was understood to mean in 1873, is not something this
chapter can answer from the statute, and the chapter should not pretend
otherwise.

## What the act does not say

Read from beginning to end, the five sections create no office, name no
person, and appoint nobody [C08-006]. There is a warrant power, given to
district and circuit judges on sworn written complaint; there is a duty on
customs officers to detain prohibited articles in the course of importation;
there is a misdemeanour for any officer or employee of the government who
aids a violation. There is no special agent, no inspectorate, no named
enforcer.

Anthony Comstock's authority, whatever it was, is not on these pages. The
chapter will have to find it somewhere else, and say where.

## Where this draft stops

Everything above comes from one document. The questions that would make this
a chapter rather than a reading of a statute — how many prosecutions there
were and for what, what the state-law and postal landscape looked like before
1873, who objected at the time and on what grounds — are Q08-3, Q08-4 and
Q08-5, and the three books that would answer them have not been acquired.

That is not a small gap. If comparable restrictions already existed in state
law and in postal regulation, the chapter's claim shifts from novelty to
consolidation, which is a different argument about the decade and a weaker
one. The draft stops before making either.

## Drafting notes, not for the chapter

- **No present-day passage, and the blocker is right that there is pressure
  for one.** The stub warns that this chapter pulls harder toward
  present-day parallels than any other. The draft answers that by staying
  inside the document: every paragraph above is a reading of printed text,
  and the reader can do what they like with it.
- **The jurisdictional reading is the chapter's argument and is sourced.**
  Federal power in 1873 reached the District, the Territories, the mails and
  the customs house, and the act uses all four. That is visible in the
  enacted text, not inferred.
- **"Unlawful abortion" must survive editing.** Dropping the qualifier would
  make the statute say something it does not say.
- **The home thread is `race`, meaning race and citizenship, and the draft
  does not yet earn it.** What is here is a federalism argument about the
  reach of national power. The connection to citizenship is presumably about
  who the national government may regulate and on what terms, and it needs
  either a sourced link or a reassignment by the author. Logged rather than
  improvised.
- **Word count:** about 1,000. The budget has this as a standard chapter at
  ~5,000.
