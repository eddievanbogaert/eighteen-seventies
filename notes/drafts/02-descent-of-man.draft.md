Darwin began the book that would make him the century's most quoted
authority on human difference by explaining why he had not wanted to write
it.

> The nature of the following work will be best understood by a brief
> account of how it came to be written. During many years I collected notes
> on the origin or descent of man, without any intention of publishing on the
> subject, but rather with the determination not to publish, as I thought
> that I should thus only add to the prejudices against my views.

That is the first sentence of *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation
to Sex*, published in two volumes by John Murray of Albemarle Street in 1871
[C02-001]. What changed his mind, he says in the next lines, was that other
naturalists had got there first and the ground had shifted under the
objection.

## A small problem with the date

The book is conventionally dated to February 1871, and this chapter cannot
confirm it. The title page gives a year and nothing more. There is no
preface. The Introduction carries no date and no place — unusual for Darwin,
who dated things. The only dated page in the volume is a publisher's
catalogue bound in at the back, headed *50a, Albemarle Street, London,
January, 1871* [C02-002], which tells you the book was bound after January
and nothing else.

This is a small thing and it is recorded because small things of this kind
are how dates get into books wrongly. The month will come from a publisher's
ledger or a dated review, not from the book.

## What the book says about races

Darwin's own account of where the argument stood is in his first paragraph.
He quotes Carl Vogt, as President of the National Institution of Geneva in
1869, saying that nobody in Europe any longer dares maintain the independent
creation of species, and takes that as evidence that the naturalists have
largely conceded descent [C02-007]. Whether that was a fair reading of the
profession in 1871 is a question for a source this chapter has not got. What
it shows is what Darwin thought he no longer had to argue.

What remained to be argued, and what chapter VII is about, is whether the
human races are one species or several — the dispute Darwin names as being
between "two schools of monogenists and polygenists" [C02-003]. He ends the
chapter by declining to settle it:

> So again it is almost a matter of indifference whether the so-called races
> of man are thus designated, or are ranked as species or sub-species; but
> the latter term appears the most appropriate. Finally, we may conclude that
> when the principles of evolution are generally accepted, as they surely
> will be before long, the dispute between the monogenists and the
> polygenists will die a silent and unobserved death. [C02-003]

It is worth noticing what kind of move that is. He does not refute the
polygenists. He predicts that evolution will make the terms of their
argument meaningless, because a theory in which species shade into one
another by gradations has no use for a bright line between kinds — and
therefore none for the classification the polygenists needed. The answer to
the question is that the question will stop existing.

Thirty-four pages earlier, in a chapter about the gap between humans and
apes, the same book says this:

> At some future period, not very distant as measured by centuries, the
> civilised races of man will almost certainly exterminate and replace
> throughout the world the savage races. [C02-004]

The sentence is not an exhortation. It appears inside a technical argument
about why the gap between man and the nearest ape is as wide as it is:
breaks in a genealogical series are made by extinctions, and Darwin expects
more extinctions, including of the great apes. It is a prediction offered as
a fact about the coming century, in a tone of complete calm.

Both sentences are Darwin's, in the same book, thirty-four pages apart. A
chapter that quotes either without the other is not describing this book.

## The last page

The book ends by arguing that nobody who has seen what humans can be should
be ashamed of the ancestry evolution gives them. The argument is carried by
one long sentence, and the sentence has a hinge in the middle of it:

> He who has seen a savage in his native land will not feel much shame, if
> forced to acknowledge that the blood of some more humble creature flows in
> his veins. For my own part I would as soon be descended from that heroic
> little monkey, who braved his dreaded enemy in order to save the life of
> his keeper; or from that old baboon, who, descending from the mountains,
> carried away in triumph his young comrade from a crowd of astonished dogs
> — as from a savage who delights to torture his enemies, offers up bloody
> sacrifices, practises infanticide without remorse, treats his wives like
> slaves, knows no decency, and is haunted by the grossest superstitions.
> [C02-005]

The monkey and the baboon are famous. They are usually quoted without the
second half of the sentence, which is the half that does the work: the point
of the heroic monkey is that it compares favourably with a particular kind of
human being, and Darwin names that human being at length.

Then the book closes, in the calmest voice in Victorian prose:

> Man still bears in his bodily frame the indelible stamp of his lowly
> origin. [C02-006]

## Where this draft stops

The chapter now has the text. It does not have the argument, and the gap
between those is the whole of Q02-3.

There is a substantial historical literature on Darwin and race, and it
disagrees with itself. The repository's bibliography names Desmond and
Moore's *Darwin's Sacred Cause* and flags it, correctly, as a position
rather than a neutral account. It has not been acquired or read, and this
chapter will not summarise an argument it has not read — nor invent a
counter-position to balance it. Under the repository's fourth rule a
disagreement is recorded by naming who holds what and saying what would
settle it, and that cannot be done from a bibliography entry.

What is needed is the literature itself, and the 1871 reception, which is
Q02-4. Until then the chapter is a reading of four passages, and says so.

## Drafting notes, not for the chapter

- **The two passages are the chapter's spine and must stay together.** The
  temptation in both directions is strong: to quote the monogenist passage
  and make Darwin an opponent of scientific racism, or to quote the
  extermination passage and make him its author. The draft refuses both by
  putting them on the same page at the same weight.
- **"Savage" and "civilised" are the book's words and appear only inside
  quotation.** No sentence of the draft's own prose uses them unquoted.
  Check this again at every revision.
- **The long sentence is quoted whole on purpose.** See the note in
  `/primary/1871-darwin-descent-vol2-conclusion.md`.
- **No present-day passage, and this chapter should probably never have
  one.** The uses made of this material in the twentieth century are a real
  subject, but they are chapter-length and they are not the 1870s.
- **Still to read:** the whole of chapter VII in images rather than OCR, and
  chapter V, which the contents summarise in part as "Evidence that civilised
  nations were once barbarous" — the thread that runs from there to the
  Fuegians on the last page is probably the chapter's real structure, and it
  has not been followed.
- **Word count:** about 1,100. The budget has this as a standard chapter at
  ~5,000.
