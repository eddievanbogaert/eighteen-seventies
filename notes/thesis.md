# Thesis

The working thesis, what it stopped claiming, and the conventions that follow
from it. Authorial decisions are recorded here as decisions; the drafting
material below is unverified and stays in `/notes` per rule 3.

## Current statement

> **Working thesis:** The 1870s installed the operating system of the modern
> world: money, race and citizenship, machines, empire.

This is the whole of it. It replaced a longer paragraph that also claimed the
decade is forgotten because nothing in it has a memorable name, together with
the illustrations that followed from that claim — the gold standard without a
slogan, the telephone as a patent dispute, Reconstruction ending in a
compromise nobody can name, the decade's biggest events sounding like
paperwork.

## Why the second claim was cut

**The thesis is now a claim about the 1870s only.**

The previous version bundled two different assertions. The first is about the
period: these four systems were installed in this decade. The second is about
the period's reception: it is neglected, and neglected *for a particular
reason*. Those are not the same kind of statement. One is a claim about what
happened; the other is a claim about what was subsequently made of what
happened, which is a claim about historiography and memory.

It was also **the only load-bearing sentence in the framing that could not be
sourced.** "Nothing in it has a memorable name" is not the sort of thing a
source establishes — it asserts an absence across the whole of later usage, and
an absence of that shape cannot be verified, only failed to be disproved. A
premise the project's own rules could never let a chapter assert is a bad
premise to hang the book on, however well it reads.

Cutting it does not mean it was wrong. It means it was doing work the thesis
should not ask it to do.

## Where the reception material goes instead

**Demoted from premise to introduction content.** It remains interesting and it
remains the reason a reader might not already know this decade. It is simply
not what the book argues.

Draft for the introduction, unverified, recorded here rather than in
`/chapters` per rule 3:

> The decade gets split in two. The years to 1877 are filed as the last act of
> Reconstruction; everything after is absorbed into "the Gilded Age," a label
> covering thirty years that conveys little beyond corruption and money.
> Neither label was built to hold the decade, and the things that matter most
> fall between them.

That is a periodization argument, and periodization arguments are answerable
from the historiography — which is what makes this a better home for the
material than the thesis line was.

### Constraint on this material

**It is an argument about periodization and historiography, not about
curricula.** Claims about what schools teach are out of scope: they vary by
country, state, decade, and syllabus, and establishing one would need a kind of
sourcing this project is not set up to do. The argument is about how the
historical literature has carved the period up and what the carving obscures.
It is not about what anyone was or wasn't taught.

If a draft sentence about the decade's neglect could only be supported by a
claim about classrooms, it is out of scope and should be cut rather than
softened.

## Conventions that follow

**The operating-system metaphor appears in the thesis statement and nowhere
else.** No extended computing figures in the prose — no patches, no reboots, no
legacy code, no installing, no running in the background. The metaphor earns
one sentence because it compresses the argument efficiently. Extended, it would
do what all extended metaphors do: start generating claims about the 1870s from
the properties of computers, which is an argument about software wearing a
history book's jacket. One use, then drop it.

**Present-day material is confined to the introduction, a coda, and at most one
deliberate passage per thread.** It does not get distributed through the
narrative chapters. Rule 6 already makes parallels framing rather than evidence
and requires them to be marked; this fixes their location as well, which rule 6
does not. Four threads means a ceiling of four in-chapter passages for the whole
book, each one a deliberate choice rather than a habit.

This supersedes the looser budget proposed in `voice-and-shape.md`, which
suggested five or six spread across the book weighted toward the front and
back. The rule above is the decision; that file has been updated to point here.

It also settles what the stubs' `## Present-day resonance` section is for. It
is a holding pen — the place a tempting parallel is parked so it can be
reviewed as rhetoric — and for most chapters the correct final state of that
section is empty.

## Decisions

**D-1 — "race and citizenship", not "citizenship". Settled.** The thesis line
carries the full thread name. The compression to "citizenship" was rejected on
two grounds: on the material, race is not a subtopic of citizenship in thread
2's chapters — for several of them the point is that citizenship was being
defined *by* race — and a reader who noticed the word missing from the one
sentence stating what the book is about would have read the omission as a
position. The author adds a second reason: the full name is also the better
rhyme with the 2020s, which is the introduction's business rather than the
thesis's, but is not a reason to weaken the thesis line.

**D-2 — "machines", not "invention". Settled.** The thread keeps its name and
the thesis line now uses it. Nothing is renamed; the README table, `AGENTS.md`,
and the five `threads: [machines]` values were already consistent and stay as
they are.

The case for "invention" is recorded in case it returns: Menlo Park is arguably
about invention becoming an industry rather than about any machine, and the
Bell chapter is about a patent, which is the legal construction of priority.
The author's reasons for keeping "machines" are stronger for this book.
Machines are the term the present-day conversation about industrialisation
actually runs on, so the word does the connective work "invention" would not;
and Butler is a live thread in that conversation rather than a period curio.

**D-3 — the first person, and Chicago. Open.** See "The first person" in
`voice-and-shape.md`. The author has personal and local grounding in Chicago
and wants to use it lightly in chapter 03. The decision is not whether that one
passage is good — it is whether this book has an authorial "I" at all, because
a first-person passage appearing once in chapter 3 with no precedent reads as
an intrusion rather than a standpoint.

## Erewhon's afterlife, and what it is not yet

Recorded as unverified recall, not as findings. The author notes that Butler is
the Butler in "Butlerian Jihad", and believes Butler surfaces in Turing and in
Orwell, with the recall explicitly marked uncertain.

This is exactly the material rule 2 exists for. It is now **Q04-5** in
`/research/QUESTIONS.md`, with each of the three claims listed separately
because each can fail separately, and none of it may be used until sourced.

If it holds, it changes chapter 04's argument. Q04-4 sets up a choice between
influence and anticipation on the basis of 1870s readership. A documented
twentieth-century afterlife is a third thing: not evidence that Erewhon mattered
in 1872, but evidence that what it saw was worth seeing. Those must not be
allowed to blur — a chapter that reaches for Dune and Turing to establish that
Butler was influential *in the 1870s* has committed the error the whole
apparatus is built to prevent. The afterlife is a reason the chapter is
interesting, not evidence about its decade.

It also weakens the proposal in `voice-and-shape.md` to merge chapters 04 and
06. A chapter that has to carry both an 1872 reading and a reception history
running to the present is not a 3,000-word chapter, and it does not fold neatly
in with Verne. That proposal is now conditional on Q04-5 coming back thin.

Under the presentism convention above, this afterlife is the obvious candidate
for the machines thread's one deliberate present-day passage. Spending it here
means not spending it on Menlo Park or the telephone.

## Consequences not yet acted on

All of the reconciliations previously listed here have been made:
`QUESTIONS.md` QX-5 and Q21-6 now describe themselves as introduction material
rather than tests of the premise; the drafting blockers in
`chapters/11-first-impressionist-exhibition.md` and
`chapters/21-specie-resumption.md` no longer invoke the cut claim. Chapter 05's
reference to names is about individuals in the scandal and was correctly left
alone.

What remains open is D-3, and the source work behind Q04-5.
