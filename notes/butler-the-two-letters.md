# Butler's two letters, and what *Erewhon* did with them

Working note for chapter 04. Not verified by the author; the rows it rests on
are `verified: "n"` in `CLAIMS.md`.

## The finding

The "Book of the Machines" is not a single argument that Butler had and then
published. It is a quarrel he had with himself in a colonial newspaper,
nine and seven years before the novel, transplanted into the novel as a
quarrel between two Erewhonians — and then settled, in the novel, in favour
of the side he had abandoned in life.

The documents:

| | date | where | position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Darwin Among the Machines", signed *Cellarius* | 13 June 1863 | *Press*, Christchurch | Machines are a new kingdom of life, evolving faster than we are. They will supersede us. Destroy them all, now. |
| "Lucubratio Ebria" | 29 July 1865 | *Press*, Christchurch | Machines are extra-corporeal limbs. Man is "a vertebrate machinate mammal". It is "a mistake … to take the view adopted by a previous correspondent of this paper". |
| *Erewhon*, chapters XXI-XXIII | 1872 | London | Both, as two Erewhonian writers. The anti-machine writer wins and the machines are destroyed. |

The 1865 essay's "previous correspondent of this paper" is Cellarius, which
is to say Butler, writing from England about a letter he had sent from a New
Zealand sheep run. He is refuting himself in public under two masks and no
contemporary reader had a way to know it.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Three things follow, and each is a different kind of claim.

**1. The prescience is older than the decade, and belongs to a sheep run.**
The argument the twentieth century remembers — that mechanical evolution runs
faster than biological, that the servant becomes the master, that the thing
is a question of time — was in print in 1863, in a four-column colonial
weekly, before the novel and before most of the technologies that make it
look prophetic. Chapter 04 cannot present 1872 as the moment of the thought.
It can present 1872 as the moment the thought reached a metropolitan
readership, which is a claim about publishing, not about insight.

**2. Butler did not believe it.** Two years after writing it he printed a
rebuttal of it. Seven years after that he put both sides in a novel and gave
the victory to the side he had rebutted — in a book that is, on its face, a
satire, in which the winning argument is attributed to a professor of
hypothetics in a country whose name is an anagram. The usual framing, that
Butler foresaw the machine age, has to survive the fact that its author
twice declined to stand behind the foresight. The better framing is that he
found the argument irresistible and untrue at the same time, which is a more
interesting condition and a more familiar one.

**3. The money thread is already inside the machines thread.** The limbs
essay reaches the conclusion that the real varieties of the human species are
no longer racial but financial, that the rich man is "the true hundred-handed
Gyges", and that "the Rothschilds are the most astonishing organisms that the
world has ever yet seen". In *Erewhon* that becomes "the great merchants and
bankers". The thing that gives a man extra limbs is money, and Butler says so
in 1865 and again in 1872. This is a documented crossing of two of the
book's four threads, not an editorial parallel, and it should be used.

## What this does to the chapter's argument

`question-triage.md` sets Q04-4 and Q04-5 as the gates, on the grounds that
the chapter does not know whether its case is 1870s readership or
twentieth-century afterlife. The two letters open a third possibility, which
is stronger than either and needs neither:

> The chapter's case is that the argument existed, in print, in a form
> essentially identical to the one we are still having, in 1863, and that its
> own author could not decide whether it was true.

That claim rests on documents in hand. It does not need Herbert, Turing or
Orwell, and it does not need sales figures. Q04-4 and Q04-5 remain worth
answering — the first because "was it read" is a fair question of any book in
a book about a decade, the second because the author asked for it — but
neither is now load-bearing, and the chapter can be drafted before they close.

## What must not be said

**Butler is not a prophet of artificial intelligence and the chapter must not
imply it.** His machines are steam engines, watches, and the *Great Eastern*.
The mechanism he imagines for their consciousness is evolutionary, not
computational; there is no calculation in it. The resemblance to present
arguments is a resemblance, it is the reason the chapter is interesting, and
under rule 6 it is framing and never evidence. The place for it is a marked
present-day passage, once D-4's allowance question is settled, and nowhere
else.

**The attribution is editorial.** "Cellarius" is identified as Butler by
Festing Jones in 1912 and Streatfeild in 1914. The self-rebuttal reading
depends entirely on that identification being right. It is not seriously
disputed, but the chapter says whose word it is.

**The text read is a reprint.** Everything above comes from a 1914 London
volume, not from the *Press*. See Q04-2.
