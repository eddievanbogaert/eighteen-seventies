## Provenance warnings, which are unusually heavy here

**The dialogue** is a 1914 reprint of a 1912 reprint of an 1862 newspaper.
Streatfeild says on printed p. 149-150 that the dialogue "was unearthed from
the early files of the PRESS, mainly owing to the exertions of Mr. Henry
Festing Jones," and was reprinted in the *Press* of 8 and 15 June 1912. The
text below is therefore at two removes from the newspaper, and the *Press*
for 1862 has not been read.

**Darwin's two notes** are transcriptions by Streatfeild of manuscripts.
Their years are in square brackets on the page — "March 24 [1863]",
"September 30 [1865]" — which is Streatfeild saying the year is his
inference, not the writer's. For the 1863 note he states the reasoning in so
many words: "The letter cannot be dated with certainty." The three "[sic]"
marks inside the 1863 note are also his.

**Neither manuscript has been seen.** Streatfeild says the autograph of the
1863 note "was purchased from Mr. Tregaskis by Mr. Festing Jones, and
subsequently presented by him to the Museum at Christ Church." Where either
is now has not been established.

## Streatfeild's prefatory note, printed page 149

> Butler arrived in New Zealand in October, 1859, and about the same time Charles Darwin's ORIGIN OF SPECIES was published. Shortly afterwards the book came into Butler's hands. He seems to have read it carefully, and meditated upon it. The result of his meditations took the shape of the following dialogue, which was published on 20 December, 1862, in the PRESS which had been started in the town of Christ Church in May, 1861. The dialogue did not by any means pass unnoticed. On the 17th of January, 1863, a leading article (of course unsigned) appeared in the PRESS, under the title "Barrel-Organs," discussing Darwin's theories, and incidentally referring to Butler's dialogue. A reply to this article, signed A.M., appeared on the 21st of February, and the correspondence was continued until the 22nd of June, 1863.

(The whole note is set in italic, with small capitals for titles; it is given
here in roman.)

## Darwin's note to an unidentified editor, printed page 150

> Down, Bromley, Kent, S.E.
> March 24 [1863].
>
> (*Private*).
>
> Mr. Darwin takes the liberty to send by this post to the Editor a New Zealand newspaper for the very improbable chance of the Editor having some spare space to reprint a Dialogue on Species. This Dialogue, written by some [sic] quite unknown to Mr. Darwin, is remarkable from its spirit and from giving so clear and accurate a view of Mr. D. [sic] theory. It is also remarkable from being published in a colony exactly 12 years old, in which it might have [sic] thought only material interests would have been regarded.

## Darwin to Butler, printed page 151

Streatfeild introduces this as a reply to Butler's pamphlet on the evidence
for the resurrection, sent to Darwin in 1865.

> Down, Bromley, Kent.
> September 30 [1865].
>
> My dear Sir,—I am much obliged to you for so kindly sending me your Evidences, etc. We have read it with much interest. It seems to me written with much force, vigour, and clearness ; and the main argument to me is quite new. I particularly agree with all you say in your preface.
>
> I do not know whether you intend to return to New Zealand, and, if you are inclined to write, I should much like to know what your future plans are.
>
> My health has been so bad during the last five months that I have been confined to my bedroom. Had it been otherwise I would have asked you if you could have spared the time to have paid us a visit ; but this at present is impossible, and I fear will be so for some time
>
> With my best thanks for your present,
>
> I remain,
> My dear Sir,
> Yours very faithfully,
> Charles Darwin.

## The dialogue opens, printed page 155, running on to 156

> [From the *Press*, 20 December, 1862.]
>
> F. So you have finished Darwin? Well, how did you like him?
>
> C. You cannot expect me to like him. He is so hard and logical, and he treats his subject with such an intensity of dry reasoning without giving himself the loose rein for a single moment from one end of the book to the other, that I must confess I have found it a great effort to read him through.
>
> F. But I fancy that, if you are to be candid, you will admit that the fault lies rather with yourself than with the book.

On printed page 156, after F. has defended the book's "judicial calmness":

> C. I admit it. Science is all head—she has no heart at all.

## Notes on the transcription, not about history

- The speakers are initials only. Over the dialogue as a whole F. expounds
  and defends Darwin and C. raises objections; the piece is an exposition
  with a reluctant interlocutor, not a debate between equals.
- Streatfeild's "about the same time" for the publication of the *Origin* is
  his phrase and is loose. The *Origin*'s publication date is not asserted
  anywhere in this file and needs its own source; it belongs to chapter 02.
- The 1862 dialogue's full text, printed pages 155-170, has been read in OCR
  and only its opening checked against the image. Anything further quoted
  from it must be checked page by page.
