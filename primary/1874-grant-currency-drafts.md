# Grant on currency, 1874 — two draft passages

**Transcription only.** Provenance for the volume is in
`1874-grant-veto-sb617.md`, including the rights position and the warning that
the OCR layer is unreliable. Both passages below are read from page images.

Both are heavily cancelled drafts. The edition's conventions, as stated in its
source note to the veto: **square brackets mark material not in Grant's hand**,
and ellipses in these texts are Grant's own. Cancellations are given
`[struck: …]`. Grant's spelling and his ampersands are reproduced as printed.

---

## Printed p. 116 — on small notes and the poorer currency

> be subjected to by a delay of the day of final settlement. It may be recollected too that this class had its *day*, in [struck: a fair p] a larger degree, on the adoption of our present financial system,[struck: — perfectly justifiable at the time] by reason of the great necessities of the Nation at the time of its adoption—

> The next step that I would propose would be that from and after the date fixed for redemption [struck: that] no bills—whether of National banks or of govt. issue—returned to the Treasury to [struck: get] be exchanged for new bills [struck: in their place] should be replaced by bills of less denomination than Ten dollars, and that in one year after resumption all bills of less denomination than Five dollars should be withdrawn from circulation, and that within two years from the same date all bills of less than Ten dollars should be so withdrawn.

> The benefit of this would be to [give] strengthen [to] the country in time of depression, whether caused by war, failure of crops or any other cause, by retaining in the hands of the people a large amount of the precious metals. All [struck: the] smaller transactions [struck: of the people] would be conducted in coin. This would give employment to many Millions of it and thereby retain it in the country. There is no question in my mind *as to the fact* that the poorer currency always will drive the better out of [struck: the market] [circulation]. With paper as a legal tender, and at a discount, gold and silver become articles of Merchandise as much as wheat or [cotton]. The surplus will find the best market it can. The balance of trade has nothing to do with the question.

## Printed p. 272 — from a draft annual message

> do not doubt their wisdom in the premises, regarding the necessity of the times—to devise a [struck: National] system of National currency, which [struck: it] proved im[struck: possible to keep] to be of a quality to keep on a par with the recognized currency of the civilized world. [struck: The latter] This begat a spirit of [struck: of] speculation—(the currency being of fluctuating value, and therefore unsafe to hold for legitimate transactions, [struck: requiring money], [naturally became a subject of speculation in itself] to present themselves)—involving an extravigance & luxury not required for the happiness or prosperity of a people, and involving, both directly & indirectly, foreign indebtedness. [struck: This debts how] These two causes however have involved us in a foreign indebtedness, contracted in good faith by borrower & lender, and should be paid—[struck: I trust all of them will be, and do not doubt for a moment the full payment of that contracted by the Nation]—in the coin, and according to the bond, agreed upon when the debt was contracted, gold, or its equivolent.

> The good faith of govt, and, [struck: American U] corporations cannot be violated towards foreign creditors without National disgrace.

"extravigance" and "equivolent" are Grant's spellings as the edition prints
them.

## How these differ from the quotations that led here

`notes/historiography-panic-1873.md` recorded that Calandro's article quotes
both passages and that its quotations would need checking against the edition.
They have been checked, and they drift. This is set down as a finding about
the secondary source, not as a complaint.

**Printed p. 116.** The article gives: "There is no question in my mind as to
the fact that the poorer currency always will drive the better out of
circulation." The page shows Grant writing "out of [struck: the market]", with
**[circulation] in square brackets — the edition's mark for material not in
Grant's hand.** The quotation therefore ends on a word that may not be
Grant's, presented as his, with the cancellation it replaced unmentioned.

**Printed p. 272.** The article gives "begat a spirit of speculation—(the
currency being of fluctuating value . . . [naturally became a subject of
speculation in itself] . . .)—involving an extravagance and luxury". Against
the page: Grant's "extravigance" is silently corrected and his ampersand
silently expanded; the ellipses stand where a cancellation and a surviving
clause were, without distinguishing between them; and the bracketed phrase is
carried inside the quotation without a note that it is not in his hand.

None of this is fabrication and none of it changes Grant's meaning much. It is
ordinary quotational tidying, of the kind most writers do without thinking.
What it demonstrates is narrower and more useful: **a quotation taken from a
secondary source arrives already smoothed, and the smoothing is invisible
until the edition is opened.** Rule 5 predicts exactly this — "paraphrase
drifts, and drift in a paraphrase is invisible" — and here is the drift,
measured.

For this book the consequence is practical. The interesting thing about these
pages is not the propositions, which are unremarkable, but the cancellations:
a man striking out "perfectly justifiable at the time", striking out "I trust
all of them will be", replacing "the market" with something. Every quotation
that reaches the book through a secondary source has had precisely that
stripped out of it.
